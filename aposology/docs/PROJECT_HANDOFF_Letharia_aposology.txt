## PROJECT OVERVIEW

Apply the absence-based framework to two closely related lichen species:
Letharia vulpina and Letharia columbiana. Use their presence/absence
patterns to understand:

1. Where each species is genuinely absent vs. under-collected
2. Whether absence patterns reveal biogeographic boundaries 
3. How relative absence (present in neighboring areas but not here)
   informs species' ecological limits
4. Whether the two species show complementary absence patterns
   (mutual exclusion zones)

This is a test case for applying aposologic methods at the species
level rather than at the community level. Two congeners with
overlapping but distinct ranges provide a controlled comparison.


## BACKGROUND: THE LETHARIA PAIR

### Letharia vulpina
- "Wolf lichen"
- Vulpinic acid chemotype (yellow-orange color)
- Generally more widespread, tolerates broader range of conditions
- Common on conifers, especially Pinus and Abies
- Range: western North America, Eurasia

### Letharia columbiana  
- "Columbia wolf lichen"
- Different secondary chemistry (no vulpinic acid, paler)
- More restricted, often at higher elevations
- Preference for Abies, sometimes on other conifers
- Range: Pacific Northwest mountains, extending south in Cascades/
  Sierra Nevada

### The Taxonomic Context
These were considered varieties of L. vulpina until recently.
L. columbiana was elevated to species rank based on chemistry
and molecular work. Some morphological overlap exists.
Records in herbaria may be mixed - older determinations may
not distinguish them properly.


## WHY THIS IS A GOOD APOSOLOGIC TEST CASE

1. **CONFINED GEOGRAPHY**: Both species largely restricted to
   western North America. No global distribution complications.

2. **OVERLAPPING BUT DISTINCT**: Ranges overlap but each has
   zones where only one occurs. Creates opportunity for mutual
   absence analysis.

3. **ELEVATION GRADIENT**: L. columbiana often at higher
   elevations. Absence at low elevation may be real ecological
   boundary vs. collecting artifact.

4. **SUBSTRATE SPECIFICITY**: Both on conifers, but L. columbiana
   more restricted. Absences in non-coniferous areas may be
   habitat-driven vs. collecting-driven.

5. **MANAGEABLE SCALE**: Can focus analysis on western states
   where both occur, rather than entire CONUS48.

6. **KNOWN CONFUSION**: Historical taxonomic confusion means
   some absences may be misidentification rather than true absence.
   Aposologic approach may detect these patterns.


## RESEARCH QUESTIONS

### Primary Questions

1. **Elevational absence patterns**: Does L. vulpina show
   consistent absence at high elevations where L. columbiana
   is present? Vice versa for L. columbiana at low elevations?

2. **Complementary absence**: Are there regions where presence
   of one correlates with absence of the other, suggesting
   competitive exclusion or distinct ecological requirements?

3. **Collector bias effects**: Do absences track collector
   effort/location, or do they show consistent elevational/
   geographic patterns independent of collecting intensity?

4. **Range boundary detection**: Can absence patterns identify
   the edges of each species' fundamental range vs. areas that
   are simply under-collected?

### Secondary Questions

5. **Chemistry-based misidentification**: Do areas with high
   L. vulpina records but zero L. columbiana records show
   patterns suggesting missed identifications?

6. **Habitat filtering**: In areas with extensive coniferous
   forest, what drives absence - climate, elevation, specific
   host preferences, or collecting gaps?

7. **Island biogeography**: In mountain ranges with both species,
   do peaks show presence/absence patterns consistent with
   habitat connectivity vs. isolation?


## ANALYTICAL APPROACH

### Step 1: Data Assembly

Extract all CLH records for both species:

```sql
SELECT id, scientificName, scientificNameRegularized,
       decimalLatitude, decimalLongitude, elevation,
       stateProvinceEd, countyEd, recordedBy, 
       institutionCode, year, substrate, 
       identifiedBy, dateIdentified
FROM narrow  
WHERE (scientificName LIKE '%Letharia vulpina%' 
       OR scientificName LIKE '%Letharia columbiana%'
       OR scientificNameRegularized LIKE '%Letharia vulpina%'
       OR scientificNameRegularized LIKE '%Letharia columbiana%')
  AND countryEd = 'United States'
  AND decimalLatitude IS NOT NULL
  AND decimalLongitude IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY stateProvinceEd, countyEd, decimalLatitude;
```

### Step 2: Geographic Framework

Use existing hex grids (33 km resolution recommended for species-level
analysis - balances resolution with sample size). For western states only:

States to include: WA, OR, CA, ID, MT, WY, UT, CO, NV, AZ, NM
Maybe: western ND, SD, NE, TX, AK

Filter hex grid files to western-only cells:

```bash
awk -F'\t' '$7 ~ /^(Washington|Oregon|California|Idaho|Montana|Wyoming|Utah|Colorado|Nevada|Arizona|New Mexico)$/' \
  /c/Lichen/hex_grids/grids/hex_33km/hex_33km_specimens.tsv > letharia_specimens.tsv
```

### Step 3: Presence/Absence Matrices

For each hex cell, determine:
- L. vulpina present (1) or absent (0)
- L. columbiana present (1) or absent (0)  
- Total Letharia specimens (any species)
- Total non-Letharia specimens (context for collecting effort)
- Number of collectors (proxy for sampling breadth)
- Elevation range in cell (from DEM or specimen records)

Create four species patterns:
1. vulpina only
2. columbiana only  
3. both species
4. neither species

### Step 4: Absence Classification

For each zero, classify confidence level:

**HIGH CONFIDENCE ABSENCE**:
- Cell has >20 total lichen specimens
- Cell has >10 Letharia specimens of the other species
- Cell has multiple collectors
- Elevation/habitat suitable for the absent species

**MEDIUM CONFIDENCE**:
- Cell has 5-20 total lichen specimens
- Cell has good collecting effort but no Letharia at all
- Single collector but extensive specimen list

**LOW CONFIDENCE** (uninformed absence):
- Cell has <5 total lichen specimens
- Cell has no collectors working on corticolous taxa
- Single record far from species' known range

### Step 5: Spatial Analysis

**Elevational gradients**: Plot presence/absence vs. elevation.
Expected pattern: L. vulpina at lower elevations, L. columbiana
higher, with transition zone.

**Geographic boundaries**: Map high-confidence absences.
Look for sharp transitions vs. gradual fadeouts.

**Collector effects**: Compare absence patterns across cells
with different collectors. If absences track collectors
rather than geography/elevation, collecting artifact.

**Complementary patterns**: Test correlation between L. vulpina
presence and L. columbiana absence, and vice versa.

### Step 6: Validation

**Known range maps**: Compare aposologic boundaries with
published range maps and monographic treatments.

**Elevation data**: Correlate absence patterns with elevation
from SRTM/NED data for each hex cell.

**Substrate availability**: Where possible, correlate absences
with forest type (coniferous vs. other) from remote sensing.

**Recent collecting**: Check iNaturalist and recent literature
to validate absence calls in well-collected areas.


## EXPECTED OUTCOMES

### If Aposologic Method Works:

1. **Clear elevational boundaries**: High-confidence L. vulpina
   absences correlate with high elevation regardless of
   collecting intensity.

2. **Complementary zones**: Areas where one species is consistently
   absent correlate with presence of the other.

3. **Range boundaries**: Sharp transitions in high-confidence
   absences mark ecological limits, not gradual fadeouts due
   to reduced collecting.

4. **Collector independence**: Absence patterns hold across
   different collectors and time periods.

### If Method Fails:

1. **Absence tracks effort**: High-confidence absences correlate
   with low collecting intensity rather than elevation/habitat.

2. **No clear boundaries**: Absences show gradual fadeouts
   consistent with reduced sampling, not ecological limits.

3. **Inconsistent patterns**: Same areas show presence for
   one collector, absence for another.

### Intermediate Cases:

4. **Mixed signals**: Some boundaries real (elevation-driven),
   others artifacts (collector-driven). Aposologic method
   helps separate them.

## POTENTIAL COMPLICATIONS

### Taxonomic Issues
- Historical confusion between varieties/species
- Records may be identified to "Letharia vulpina s.l."
- Need to flag ambiguous determinations

### Geographic Scope
- Alaska populations may behave differently
- Mexican records exist but coverage sparse
- Focus on western CONUS48 for consistency

### Elevation Artifacts
- High-elevation areas may be systematically under-collected
- Seasonal access issues for alpine sites
- Collecting bias toward accessible elevations

### Substrate Limitations
- Both species are conifer specialists
- Absence in grassland/desert may be uninformative
- Need to mask non-forest areas or weight accordingly

### Scale Effects
- 33 km cells may be too coarse for elevational transitions
- 10 km cells may have too many zero-specimen cells
- May need to test multiple grid resolutions


## CONNECTION TO BROADER APOSOLOGIC FRAMEWORK

This project tests the absence-based approach at the species level
rather than community level. Success here validates the method
for single-species distribution modeling. Failure identifies
limitations or scale dependencies.

Key methodological contributions:

1. **Binary species framework**: Simpler than multi-species
   community analysis, easier to validate against known ranges.

2. **Confidence-weighted zeros**: Develops criteria for
   distinguishing informed vs. uninformed absences at
   species level.

3. **Relative absence at species boundaries**: Tests whether
   "present everywhere nearby but not here" is a reliable
   indicator of ecological limits.

4. **Collector calibration**: Determines whether collectors
   show consistent bias patterns for these species across
   multiple areas.

Success with Letharia pair opens the door to broader single-species
aposologic analyses across the CLH database.
